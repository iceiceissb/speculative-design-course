Bodystorming through disruption:
 AI-assisted design improvisation pedagogy

Jiawei Lia, Jiayue Wangb, Lin Zhua, Jiaxuan Xua, Changran Zhaoc , Zhiyong Fua*
aTsinghua University,People's Republic of China
bBeijing Institute of Fashion Technology,China

         Abstract:  This study  introduces an  AI-assisted Design  Improvisation Pedagogy
          integrating  artificial  intelligence with bodystorming  in space design education.
        Grounded   in  a   three-dimensional  framework  (Embodiment,   Enactment,
         Improvisation), an  AI  system  using  Coze  platform  with  Retrieval-Augmented
        Generation (RAG) was developed to support emergent scenario generation and
         improvisational guidance. Validated through three-semester workshops with 16 third-
         year undergraduates working on space environment challenges (crystal formation,
          fiber construction, plant growth, sound materialization), the system demonstrated
         highest correlation with Improvisation (r=0.48). Students consistently underestimated
          their performance versus peer evaluations, particularly in Improvisation where all
        groups showed significant differences (p<0.01). Different materials produced varied
         impacts, with Sound group showing largest gaps due to abstract media nature.
         Findings confirm AI's transition from assistive tool to creative collaborator, offering
         novel pedagogical approaches for extreme environment design.

        Keywords: AI-assisted pedagogy; bodystorming; design improvisation; design
         education

1. Introduction

1.1  Research Background
Design education fundamentally concerns fostering innovative thinking and
capabilities.   Traditional approaches emphasize creativity through physical movements,
prototype construction, and situated experiences.
Bodystorming  is an embodied ideation method that extends brainstorming into
physical space, requiring participants to enact scenarios using their bodies rather
than relying solely on verbal or visual abstraction.  Originating in the mid-1990s
from the field of participatory and contextual design (Burns et  al., 1994),
bodystorming enables designers to transcend conventional cognitive patterns by

simulating bodily interactions in authentic scenarios, transforming abstract concepts
into tangible solutions (Oulasvirta et  al., 2003).   Its key characteristics include multi-
sensory engagement (visual, auditory, tactile, kinesthetic), direct spatial immersion,
and the activation of tacit knowledge that  is difficult to surface through conventional
methods.  Typical applications include acting out a hospital waiting room experience
to identify pain points in service design, or simulating microgravity movement
constraints to inform spatial layout decisions in aerospace habitability research.
Schon's (1983) "reflective practice" theory articulates that design thinking essence lies
in "reflection-in-action," with bodily engagement as primary medium.  Such physical
engagement enriches learning and catalyzes design thinking through embodied
interaction (Klemmer et  al., 2006).
Design improvisation refers to the capacity to generate creative responses to
unexpected situations in real time, drawing on embodied knowledge, contextual
awareness, and collaborative dynamics.  Unlike scripted performance, improvisation in
design education embraces uncertainty as a productive force: moderate constraints
stimulate creative release (Amabile, 1996), while Csikszentmihalyi's (1997) "flow"
theory explains how designers enter peak creative states when challenges align with
their capabilities.  Dorst's (2011) research reveals that experienced designers make
appropriate improvisational judgments in complex situations based on prior
experience.  Together, bodystorming and design improvisation form a complementary
pedagogical dyad: the former provides the embodied scaffold, the latter activates
adaptive thinking within  it.  This combination  is particularly valuable in extreme
environment design contexts, where standard usability methods are insufficient and
designers must rely on imagined, embodied simulation.

1.2  Course Framework: "Design for Space"
Over three years, our undergraduate "Interaction Design" course focused on space design
challenges. With commercial spaceflight advancement, designers must rethink human
survival in extreme environments. This course guides students in exploring innovative
solutions for space habitation systems, life support equipment, and extraterrestrial human-
machine interactions, considering technical feasibility, resource sustainability, and
unpredictable adaptability.
Following the Double Diamond model (Design Council, 2005), the course structures four
phases (Figure 1):
         Strategy (STEEP analysis, Futures Triangle);
         Design (Design Fiction,
         bodystorming workshops);
         Technology (prototype iteration);
         Economy (practical application demonstration).
This paper focuses on AI-assisted improvisation workshops during the Design phase, with
third-year students who completed foundational design training, conducted over spring and
fall semesters with 20 students per semester in groups of 4-6.

1.3  Research Objectives
This research explores two core questions: First, how can AI as "collaborator"
enhance students' performance in embodied interaction, scenario enactment, and
improvised adaptation through diverse scenarios and movement prompts? Second,
how does human-AI collaborative improvisation influence individual and team creative
thinking, particularly in spatial awareness, scenario construction, and improvisational
creativity?

2. Literature Review

2.1   Design Improvisation
Bodystorming transcends linguistic and cognitive limitations of traditional brainstorming
(Burns et al., 1994; Schleicher et al., 2010) by extending design thinking to the physical
realm, requiring direct participation, movement simulation, and scenario enactment
(Oulasvirta et al., 2003). It enables more immediate experiential understanding than
observation and interviews (Buchenau & Suri, 2000), particularly for needs difficult to
articulate verbally (Schleicher et al., 2010). Applications span service design, interaction
design, and spatial design (Marquez Segura et al., 2016; Colombetti, 2014).
Design improvisation emphasizes embodied cognition's central role in creative processes,
requiring rapid contextual responses and decisions. Dorst's (2011) research reveals
experienced designers make appropriate improvisational judgments in complex situations
based on prior experience. Unexpected situations critically trigger improvisation: moderate
constraints stimulate creative release (Amabile, 1996), while excessive pressure reduces
performance (Byron et al., 2010). Csikszentmihalyi's (1997) "flow" theory explains creators
enter creative states when challenges match capabilities. Improvisation stimulates creative
thinking, breaks conventional patterns (Cross, 2006), and cultivates adaptability (Lawson,
2006).

2.2  AI-assisted Innovation
AI in design education has evolved from "auxiliary tool" to "creative collaboration
partner." With GANs and large language models maturity, AI demonstrates unique
value in design improvisation through two dimensions:  First, scenario construction—
rapidly generating diverse prompts to explore broader possibility spaces. Hao et  al.
(2024) explored AI applications in urban scenario planning across solution generation,
scenario construction, and evaluation. Second, real-time interaction—serving as
dialogic tool providing personalized explanations and continuous feedback.
Empirical research confirms AI's positive role: Hwang and Wu's (2025) survey of 121
design students found generative AI significantly impacted creative cognition (β=0.610,
p<0.001) through enhanced self-efficacy and reduced anxiety. Xu and Jiang's (2022)
research demonstrated AI teaching methods outperformed traditional approaches in
flexibility, outcomes, and engagement.
In improvisation performance, Carter et  al. (2021) found AI expands creative
possibilities and helps break mental patterns through continuous dialogue. Mirowski
and Mathewson (2019) showed AI as performance partner forces focus on narrative
and emotional expression. These studies indicate AI as interactive, inspirational
collaborator offers new possibilities for Design Improvisation Pedagogy.

3. Workshop Implementation

3.1  Preparation

3.1.1  Space Design Scenario Selection

The selection of space environment scenarios was driven by both pedagogical and
disciplinary rationale. Space environments present conditions—microgravity, radiation,
confined habitats, psychological isolation—that are radically different from everyday
experience, making them ideal for testing the limits of embodied imagination and
improvisational design thinking. Exposing students to such extreme contexts compels
them to abandon familiar cognitive shortcuts and engage in genuine scenario-building
from the body outward. The three scenarios (International Space Station, Lunar Base
Station, and Mars Base Station) were chosen to represent a spectrum of human
distance from Earth, each escalating in terms of resource constraints, communication
latency, and psychological demand. These environments directly target the
Improvisation dimension of our framework by creating conditions where standard
responses are inadequate and adaptive creativity  is required.

The four research directions—plant growth, crystal formation, sound materialization,
and fiber construction—were selected to engage distinct cognitive and sensorimotor
modalities. Plant growth and crystal formation involve slow biological and chemical
processes that challenge students to translate invisible temporal dynamics into visible,
bodily enacted narratives. Sound materialization requires engagement with abstract,

non-visual media, demanding higher-order bodily metaphor. Fiber construction, with
its structural and haptic complexity, foregrounds spatial and tactile reasoning.
Together, these directions were designed to cultivate different facets of the three-
dimensional framework: Embodiment (physical simulation of material properties),
Enactment (narrative construction around scientific processes), and Improvisation
(adaptive problem-solving when these processes encounter unexpected disruptions).
We used AI to generate diverse situational prompts breaking students' cognitive
limitations, providing new creative pathways for ideation (Meyer & Norman, 2020).

3.1.2  AI System Design

We developed an AI-assisted system on Coze platform utilizing Agent-building
modules, workflow architecture, and ChatFlow functionalities, implementing two core
functions:
Emergent Scenario Module (Figure 2): Workflow-based architecture with Story
Generator and Story Distiller nodes. System receives  initial scene description, LLM
parses key narrative elements, Story Distiller processes content forwarding to RAG
module. RAG integrates Theoretical Basis Node and BingWebResearch for dual-layer
validation ensuring scientific accuracy. Scenario Generator uses pre-configured
prompts generating five emergent events significantly affecting narrative trajectory.





Figure 2. Workflow Diagram of the Emergent Scenario Generation Module within the AI-
         Assisted Improvisation System

Improvisational Performance Module (Figure 3): ChatFlow architecture automatically
converting Story Generator outputs to system inputs. Two-layer validation:
BingWebResearch retrieves academic literature; Solution Generator assesses domain
relevance, practical feasibility, pedagogical applicability. Prompt design incorporates
domain knowledge accuracy, objective declarative language style, and structured
guidance preserving creative improvisation space.
Iterative development optimized performance through multiple testing rounds.  Initial
debugging revealed Improvisational Module prone to hallucinations without RAG,

3.2  Workshop Process
The workshop was structured around the three-dimensional competency framework
(Embodiment, Enactment, Improvisation) with AI positioned as an "Out-Actor"—an
external agent that disrupts and enriches the creative process from outside the
performance circle, rather than replacing student agency within  it. The overall
structure followed a "Design Fiction–Design Reflection" bidirectional pathway:
students  first constructed and enacted speculative scenarios, then reflected on and
revised those scenarios in light of AI-generated disruptions. Instructors served as
facilitators and observers, guiding the reflective debriefs, calibrating the difficulty of
AI interventions in consultation with the pedagogical framework, and ensuring that AI
outputs were educationally appropriate. Students were the primary creative agents,
responsible for interpreting and embodying scenario scripts, selecting and
transforming AI-generated emergent events into physical performance, and evaluating
peer groups using structured rubrics.

(Table 1) assessing baseline competencies across three dimensions on a 5-point Likert
scale. This  initial assessment served two purposes:  it helped instructors tailor AI
prompt complexity to each group's starting level, and  it established a baseline for
measuring self-perception gaps after the workshop.

3.2.2  Embodied Improvisational Enactment

The enactment phase followed a four-level progressive structure (Figure 4), each
level building on the previous to incrementally deepen human-AI collaborative
improvisation.
Basic Scenario Enactment: One group performed a 10-minute live enactment following their
prewritten script, while three observer groups documented the performance using
structured observation sheets.
AI-Assisted Intervention: Observer groups input the performing group's script summary into
the Emergent Scenario Module. The AI processed the narrative context through its RAG-
enhanced pipeline and generated five scientifically grounded emergent events—such as
sudden equipment failure, unexpected biological anomalies, or communication blackouts—
each with multiple branching response suggestions. Crucially, student observers, not
instructors, operated the AI interface. This design choice made students active co-creators of

the disruption scenarios, deepening their engagement with both the AI system and the
content. The AI outputs were presented to all groups simultaneously and discussed briefly
before the next performance phase began.
Solution Translation: The performing group selected one emergent event from the AI's
output and used the Improvisational Performance Module to develop embodied response
strategies. Students translated abstract AI-generated technical solutions into physical
performance actions—for example, converting a recommendation about "semantic isolation
algorithms" into a choreographed sequence of bodily gestures representing information
triage. The Improvisational Performance Module supported this translation by providing
structured, encyclopedic guidance on relevant scientific principles while explicitly leaving the
physical interpretation open to student creativity. Iteration was encouraged: if a group's
initial embodied response felt disconnected from the scenario logic, they could re-query the
system or adjust their physical framing.
Cross-Group Interaction: Observer groups provided real-time documentation, verbal
feedback, and completed peer evaluation forms after each round. Groups rotated roles
across sessions, ensuring every group experienced both performing and observing positions.

4. Results and Analysis

Participants: 16 third-year undergraduate design students (average age 21) with completed
foundational training. Two structured questionnaires: Individual Self-Assessment (nine items,
5-point Likert scale across three dimensions) and Team Performance Evaluation (mirrored
structure plus three AI-specific items).
Data processed using Python 3.11.8 (numpy, statsmodels, matplotlib): Cronbach's alpha for
reliability, descriptive statistics for mean scores and standard deviations, Pearson correlation
analysis, paired-sample t-tests for self versus peer evaluation differences.

lower (α=0.34, α=0.59). Peer evaluation: greater consistency—Improvisation α=0.74,
Enactment α=0.62, Embodiment α=0.58, AI dimension α=0.70.

6. Discussion

6.1  Key Findings
Students consistently underestimated their performance relative to peer evaluations, a
pattern potentially reflecting a reverse Dunning-Kruger dynamic in creative contexts:
performers immersed in technical execution focus on difficulties and failures, while
evaluators assess holistic effect and emergent narrative coherence. Self-critical
awareness and divergent evaluation criteria further widen this gap. Improvisation
showed the most consistent underestimation  (all four groups significant), reflecting
that adaptive creative competence  is inherently difficult to self-assess in the moment
—it becomes visible to observers as responsive fluency, but  is experienced internally
as reactive struggle. Embodiment showed smaller differences (25% significant), as
physical actions are more directly perceivable by the performer.
Material type significantly shaped the self-peer gap pattern. The Sound group
showed significant differences across  all three dimensions, attributable to the abstract





                                            14

===== PDF PAGE 17 =====

temporal nature of sound as a medium—without tangible physical anchors, students
found  it harder to assess their own spatial and narrative coherence, while observers
recognized strong improvisational creativity in their efforts to materialize the
intangible. The Fiber group's Enactment difference reflects the cognitive challenge of
communicating complex engineering thinking through physical metaphor. The Plant
group's largest Improvisation gap indicates that rapid interdisciplinary knowledge
integration—bridging biology, cognitive science, and design—exceeded students' self-
recognition capacity. These patterns reveal a strong connection between material
cognitive characteristics and students' metacognitive awareness, suggesting that
teaching strategies should be adapted to the specific epistemic demands of each
material type.

6.2  Innovative Value
This study makes contributions to both design education methodology and extreme
environment design practice. In terms of pedagogical method,  it confirms AI's role as
a "collaborative partner" that shifts the instructional dynamic from one-way content
delivery to bidirectional creative dialogue. AI-driven emergent scenarios provide
scientifically validated, diverse, and unpredictable challenges that break habitual
design patterns. The highest correlation with Improvisation (r=0.48) confirms that this
disruption effect  is most pronounced in the domain of adaptive creativity. The RAG
mechanism was essential in ensuring scientific accuracy and avoiding hallucinations;
without  it, AI outputs risked producing speculative scenarios disconnected from actual
aerospace science, undermining students' trust in the system. Multi-path prompts
actively encouraged divergent thinking, and the system supported personalized
learning pathways by adapting prompting strategies to different performance levels
and material characteristics.
Beyond design education, this approach holds relevance for other domains that rely
on scenario-based learning under constraint, including healthcare simulation,
emergency response training, and cross-disciplinary innovation workshops. The
integration of AI as an adaptive scenario engine—rather than a static content
provider—offers a replicable model for any educational context where improvisation,
embodied cognition, and uncertainty tolerance are core learning objectives. For
design fields specifically, the method could be extended to industrial design
(simulating user interactions with novel products), architectural design (enacting
inhabitant behaviors in speculative spatial conditions), or service design (embodying
complex system failures in real-time). In each case, AI's capacity to generate
scientifically grounded, narratively rich disruptions could activate the same
improvisational dynamics observed in this study.

6.3  Limitations
Three limitations affect the generalizability and depth of this study. First, the sample
size was small (16 students, 4 groups) from a single discipline and specific cognitive
level. Low reliability in some self-assessment dimensions (Cronbach's alpha=0.34 for
Embodiment) may reflect insufficient sample size rather than construct invalidity.

Future studies should expand to larger, more diverse student populations across
disciplines to enhance external validity and allow for more robust statistical
inference.
Second, the AI model, while RAG-enhanced, relies on existing academic literature and
web data rather than real space environment data. The black-box nature of LLM
generation renders the reasoning logic opaque, potentially compromising trust and
pedagogical transparency. The system may also lack depth in highly specialized
aerospace engineering problems that  fall outside  its training distribution. Future
iterations should introduce expert validation loops and rigorous quality control
protocols to ensure that AI-generated scenarios meet domain accuracy standards.
Third, conventional classroom environments could not authentically replicate the
embodied conditions of space (microgravity, radiation, confined spaces), limiting the
fidelity of physical enactments. Limited props and spatial configurations constrained
realistic performance. Future research should explore VR/AR integration to provide
more immersive and accurate extreme environment simulation, and investigate how
increased environmental fidelity affects both performance patterns and AI-human
interaction dynamics.

6.4  Future Work
Future research should develop across four interconnected dimensions.  First,
methodological optimization should focus on integrating VR and AR technologies to
overcome the current gap between classroom simulation and actual space conditions.
The absence of physical anchors such as microgravity or spatial confinement limited
the realism of student enactments; immersive environments would not only improve
fidelity but potentially activate different embodied responses and deepen the
Embodiment dimension specifically. Standardized, cross-disciplinary assessment tools
should also be developed to enable comparative evaluation across different design
contexts and material types.
Second, AI system advancement should prioritize two areas: building expert
knowledge bases co-developed with aerospace scientists and domain specialists, and
developing interpretable reasoning mechanisms that make AI generative logic visible
to students and instructors. The latter  is particularly important pedagogically—when
students understand why the AI generated a particular disruption event, they can
engage with  it as a learning object rather than merely a performance constraint.
Personalized learning algorithms that adapt scenario complexity and prompting style
in real time to individual student performance profiles represent a promising
direction for maximizing both cognitive challenge and creative support.
Third, cross-domain expansion should test whether the AI-assisted improvisation
framework generalizes beyond space design. Candidate domains include architectural
design (enacting speculative inhabitant behaviors), healthcare design (simulating
complex patient journey disruptions), and emergency response training (practicing
adaptive decision-making under simulated crisis conditions). Each domain would
provide a different profile of material cognitive demands, allowing systematic

comparison of how AI-generated disruptions interact with different knowledge
structures and embodied repertoires.
Fourth, longitudinal tracking studies should evaluate whether the improvisational
creativity and adaptive problem-solving capacities cultivated in these workshops
transfer to longer-term professional development outcomes. Tracking students' design
practice over one to three years following the workshop would illuminate whether
AI-assisted bodystorming produces durable shifts in creative cognition, or primarily
delivers in-the-moment performance effects.

7. Conclusion
This study validated an AI-assisted design improvisation pedagogy integrating AI with
bodystorming in space design education. Three key findings emerged: AI-driven
emergent scenarios effectively broke habitual patterns, with the highest correlation
observed in Improvisation (r=0.48); students consistently underestimated their
performance, especially in Improvisation where  all groups showed significant
differences; and different materials produced differentiated cognitive and performance
impacts, with Sound showing the largest gap due to abstract media nature and Plant
exhibiting the greatest Improvisation disparity from biological complexity.
The theoretical contribution of this work lies in enriching design education
methodology by validating AI's shift from assistive tool to creative collaborator, and
in proposing an innovative pedagogical model that integrates embodied cognition,
reflective practice, and AI-generated disruption. Practically, this study offers a new
methodological pathway for extreme environment design education, demonstrating
how AI can support improvisational creativity and problem-solving in constrained
environments through collaborative partnership rather than mere technical assistance.
Despite the limitations of sample size, AI transparency, and environmental simulation
fidelity, this study provides valuable insights for the development of intelligent design
education and opens productive avenues for applying embodied, AI-augmented
improvisation across diverse design disciplines and extreme contexts.
